GPU surfaces (zero-copy)¶

A local client that renders on the GPU, a video player or a drawing canvas, can share its output with the terminal without a single CPU copy by transmitting a GPU surface, t=g. The payload is the name of the surface: an IOSurface on macOS and a dma-buf on Linux, as below. Other kinds of surface are refused with EINVAL, as is t=g on platforms without an implementation. Chunking (m) and compression (o) are not allowed with t=g.

On macOS the payload is iosurface:<id> where <id> is the integer returned by IOSurfaceGetID(). The surface must be created with kIOSurfaceIsGlobal set, otherwise IOSurfaceLookup() in the terminal cannot find it. The surface must be 32-bit BGRA or RGBA with straight (not pre-multiplied) alpha, as for f=32. The f, s and v keys are ignored, the dimensions and pixel format come from the surface itself.

On Linux it is a dma-buf, the kind of buffer that GPU drivers, Vulkan, GBM and udmabuf share between processes. A dma-buf is a file descriptor and has no name that another process could look up, so the payload names the process that holds it and its descriptors there:

dmabuf:<pid>:<fourcc>:<modifier>:<fd>,<offset>,<stride>[:<fd>,<offset>,<stride>...]
<pid>

The id of the process that holds the buffer, as the terminal sees it, so a client in a container with process ids of its own cannot use this.

<fourcc>

The DRM format, as the four characters of its code. AB24 (DRM_FORMAT_ABGR8888) has the bytes R, G, B, A in memory, the order of f=32, and AR24 (DRM_FORMAT_ARGB8888) has B, G, R, A. XB24 and XR24 are the same with the fourth byte ignored. Alpha is straight, as for f=32.

<modifier>

The DRM format modifier of the buffer, in decimal or in hexadecimal after 0x: 0 for a linear buffer, 0x00ffffffffffffff (DRM_FORMAT_MOD_INVALID) to leave the layout to the driver, or the one the allocator chose.

<fd>,<offset>,<stride>

One to four planes, each the number of the descriptor in the process that holds the buffer, the offset of the plane in it and its stride in bytes, as the allocator gives them. The formats above have one plane, a modifier can add more.

A dma-buf does not know its size, so s and v are required and give the width and height in pixels; f is ignored.

The terminal takes duplicates of the descriptors with pidfd_getfd(2), as a debugger could, and only from a process in the session of the program the window runs: the shell of the window and everything started from it. Any other process is refused, so that a program cannot have the terminal show the buffers of another window or of a program that is not its own. The kernel adds a condition of its own, that the terminal be allowed to trace the process. With Yama’s ptrace_scope at 0 or 1, the default of most distributions, that holds for the processes the terminal started; at 2 or 3 it holds for none, and every dma-buf is refused. A descriptor that is not a dma-buf is refused too. The terminal closes its duplicates once the buffer is imported, the driver keeping the buffer for as long as the image exists, and the client may close its own descriptors whenever it likes after the reply.

The buffer is imported with EGL_EXT_image_dma_buf_import, and EGL_EXT_image_dma_buf_import_modifiers for a modifier other than 0 and 0x00ffffffffffffff, so it works where kitty draws with EGL: under Wayland, and not under X11, where it draws with GLX. Every refusal is an EINVAL with a message that says why: under X11, a payload that is not as above, no s or v, a process outside the session, a descriptor that cannot be taken or is not a dma-buf, or a driver that cannot import the buffer. A query, a=q, does all of it but the import, so its answer says whether the terminal could take the buffer.

When it copies a dma-buf, the terminal relies on the synchronization that the drivers of dma-bufs do implicitly for the rendering the client submitted before it sent the command. A client whose driver does not, or that writes the buffer with the CPU, finishes its writes before it sends the command.

This was tried on Ubuntu 26.04 with Mesa’s software renderer, under a headless sway, with buffers made from memory with udmabuf in the formats AB24 and XR24 and the modifiers 0 and 0x00ffffffffffffff: the content of the buffer showed, and after a refresh its new content. It has not been tried with buffers of a GPU driver or with other modifiers.

The terminal keeps the surface for as long as the image exists, and copies it into the image on the GPU when it is transmitted and again on every refresh, so the image shows what the surface held at the last of those. After updating the surface, the client sends the refresh action:

<ESC>_Ga=r,i=31<ESC>\

which copies the surface again and marks the image as changed so that the next frame shows the new content. The terminal responds OK unless responses are suppressed with q. Sending a=t or a=T again with t=g and the same surface for an existing image id is equivalent to a refresh followed by the usual placement. Deleting the image releases the surface. Animation frames (a=f) and composition (a=c) are not supported for surface backed images, the terminal responds with EINVAL. a=q with t=g can be used to check for support, a terminal that does not know the medium responds with an error.

Relays such as multiplexers or Goblin Skiff that forward a terminal session to another machine cannot pass the surface along, but they can read it. On macOS any process of the same user can call IOSurfaceLookup() with the id, then IOSurfaceLock() and IOSurfaceGetBaseAddress() to read the pixels. On Linux a relay that started the client can take the descriptors with pidfd_getfd(2) as the terminal does, and read the pixels of a linear buffer with mmap(2); the terminal itself refuses a dma-buf named through a multiplexer, whose clients are not in the session of the window. A relay can poll the surface, for instance on every refresh action, and forward the pixels to the far side with an ordinary t=d transmission.

The base transmission, placement, deletion, and reply rules are specified by the kitty graphics protocol.
